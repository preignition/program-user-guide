# Rooted in the Disability Movement

The platform's accessibility choices are not a modern product trend. They come from a movement that changed how disability is understood, who gets to speak about it, and what a just society owes its members. This article explains that history and the principles it hands to anyone building a survey.

## From charity to rights

For much of the twentieth century, disability was framed as a medical problem to be treated or a personal misfortune to be pitied. Services were designed by professionals, often without the people they served. The result was exclusion dressed as care: separate schools, sheltered workshops, inaccessible buildings, and information available only in formats institutions found convenient.

Persons with disabilities organized against that framing. The **social model** of disability, developed in the disability movement, distinguished impairment from disability: impairment is a characteristic; disability is the barrier society places in front of it. The problem moved from the body to the built and social world.

This history is not a footnote to accessibility. It explains why the movement's slogan, **"Nothing about us without us"**, is a design requirement rather than a sentiment.

## The Convention

The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), adopted in 2006 and in force since 2008, turned that movement's demands into binding human rights language. Its principles (Article 3) include:

- respect for inherent dignity, individual autonomy and independence;
- non-discrimination;
- full and effective participation and inclusion in society;
- respect for difference and acceptance of persons with disabilities as part of human diversity;
- equality of opportunity;
- accessibility.

Two obligations follow for anyone producing information or services:

- **Article 9** requires accessibility of information, communications and electronic services.
- **Article 21** requires information for the general public in accessible formats and technologies, in a timely manner and without additional cost, and recognizes the use of sign languages.

A survey is exactly the kind of information and service these articles describe.

## Participation

Article 3(c) requires full and effective participation and inclusion. Article 4(3) requires States Parties to consult closely with and actively involve persons with disabilities, including children with disabilities, in decisions about policies and programmes that concern them.

Applied to research, this means:

- persons with disabilities are participants in designing the survey, not only subjects answering it;
- Easy Read materials are produced with people with intellectual disabilities, not merely for them;
- sign-language videos are reviewed by Deaf people who use that language;
- the wording of questions is tested with the communities it describes.

A survey written about a community without that community's involvement tends to reveal more about the authors than about the respondents.

## Language matters

The disability movement changed vocabulary, and the Convention codifies it:

- **"Persons with disabilities"**, not "the disabled", "handicapped" or "special needs".
- **"Accessibility"**, not "disabled access".
- **"Reasonable accommodation"** for individual adjustments.
- **"Universal design"** for designs usable by everyone.
- **Signed languages** are languages (Article 2), not gestures or pantomime.

Person-first wording ("a person with low vision") is the Convention's default in English. Some communities prefer identity-first wording ("a Deaf person"), and that preference should be respected. The rule is simple: use the wording the community uses for itself, and do not turn a characteristic into a definition.

Avoid the adjacent traps:

- **Inspiration framing** — presenting ordinary participation as heroic.
- **Suffering framing** — "suffers from", "afflicted by", "wheelchair-bound".
- **Medicalized labels** where a functional description is meant.
- **False universals** — "everyone knows", "normal users".

## What this means for a survey

1. **Ask who is missing.** Before fielding, list the groups the survey's design may exclude and why.
2. **Budget for participation.** Review by persons with disabilities is part of the research cost, not a courtesy at the end.
3. **Do not separate the accessible sample.** Use the same instrument and the same dataset.
4. **Make the call for participation accessible.** An inaccessible invitation excludes respondents before the first question.
5. **Report limitations honestly.** If the design excluded a group, say so; do not generalize from the people who could take part.

## A living movement

The Convention is a floor, not a ceiling. Accessibility standards change, assistive technology changes, and communities' expectations change. The platform's documentation follows the Convention's terminology and the communities' own usage; when the two differ, the community's preference wins.
