# We Have Different Needs

A survey that works for "the average respondent" works for nobody in particular. Respondents differ in how they see, hear, move, read, remember, communicate and use technology. Some differences are permanent, some temporary, some situational — a broken wrist, a noisy train, a language learned later in life. An accessible survey assumes that difference is normal.

This is the first principle behind the platform, and the one that shapes the others.

## Disability is an interaction, not a defect

The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) defines persons with disabilities as those with long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairments which, **in interaction with various barriers**, may hinder full and effective participation on an equal basis with others (Article 1). The preamble is explicit: disability results from the interaction between persons with impairments and attitudinal and environmental barriers.

That definition moves the question from "what is wrong with this person?" to "what is in the way?"

For a survey, the barriers are concrete:

- a question that relies on color alone;
- a video with no captions or sign language;
- a consent statement written at postgraduate reading level;
- a drag-and-drop control that cannot be operated by keyboard;
- a timer that punishes a respondent who reads slowly;
- an email-only invitation for someone without an inbox.

None of these is about the respondent's capacity. Each is a design decision that can be made differently.

## One survey, several routes

Because needs differ, the platform offers more than one way through the same questionnaire:

- **Read Aloud** for respondents who listen rather than read.
- **Easy Read** for respondents who need simplified language and images.
- **Sign Language** for Deaf respondents whose first language is a signed language.
- **Voice Recording** for respondents who speak more easily than they type.
- **Plain-language tooltips** for terms that cannot be removed but can be explained.
- **Keyboard and screen-reader support** throughout the interface.
- **Email commands** in the Listserv, for people whose connectivity or assistive setup makes a web form impractical.

These are not separate surveys. They are routes into the same data, so a respondent who uses Easy Read is counted with everyone else rather than in a side channel.

## Autonomy and reasonable accommodation

CRPD Article 3 opens with respect for inherent dignity, individual autonomy — including the freedom to make one's own choices — and independence. Accessibility is not about deciding for people what they need. It is about removing barriers so they can choose.

Two ideas from the Convention are useful here:

- **Universal design** means designing products and environments usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without adaptation (Article 2).
- **Reasonable accommodation** means the specific adjustments a person needs in a particular situation (Article 2), such as an alternative format or extra time.

A good survey does both: it is broadly usable out of the box, and it allows the individual adjustments a particular respondent needs.

## What this means in practice

- **Ask about formats, not just answers.** If a respondent cannot perceive a question, the answer is not the problem.
- **Do not make accessibility a separate questionnaire.** A separate "accessible version" fragments the sample and signals that some respondents are an exception.
- **Test with the people who will answer.** A design that seems accessible to its author is a hypothesis, not evidence.
- **Treat difficulty as a finding.** If respondents drop off at a question, the question — not the respondent — is the first suspect.
